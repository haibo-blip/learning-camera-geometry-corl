Our goal is to train an RGB policy that can use camera geometry without requiring calibrated camera poses at test time.  The central design is to make camera pose an internal latent variable: a pose predictor estimates the camera-to-world transform of each input view, the estimated transforms are converted into per-pixel ray features, and the downstream policy is trained end-to-end through these geometric features.  As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, we instantiate this idea in two variants.  The first variant trains an action policy directly from predicted camera geometry.  The second variant adds a scene-token novel-view synthesis objective that encourages the observation representation to preserve 3D scene structure.

\subsection{Problem Setup}

At time \(t\), the policy observes \(N\) RGB views
\(\mathcal{I}_t=\{I_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\), proprioception \(q_t\), and a task
condition \(c\) such as a language embedding.  It predicts an action chunk
\(A_t=\{a_t,\ldots,a_{t+H-1}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_a}\).  In our
implementation \(H=32\) and each action contains end-effector translation,
6D rotation, and gripper command.  Camera \(i\) has intrinsics \(K_i\) and an
extrinsic transform \(T_{t,i}\in SE(3)\).  We do not assume that \(T_{t,i}\)
is available for every training episode.  Pose annotation is assigned at the
episode level: for episode \(e\), a binary mask \(m_e\in\{0,1\}\) indicates
whether all camera poses in that episode are labeled.  The label ratio
\(\rho\) is therefore the fraction of training episodes with camera-pose
annotations.

The policy should therefore solve two coupled problems.  It must infer enough
camera geometry to express pixels in a shared spatial frame, and it must use
that representation to predict actions.  This coupling is important: when
\(\rho\) is small, camera-pose supervision alone is insufficient, so the action
and reconstruction objectives must also shape the pose predictor and the
observation encoder.

\subsection{Semi-Supervised Camera-Pose Prediction}

Given the RGB views, a pose head \(h_\phi\) predicts a transform
\(\hat{T}_{t,i}=h_\phi(\mathcal{I}_t)_i\) for every input camera.  The
supervised pose loss is applied only to timesteps and views from labeled
episodes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}} =
\frac{1}{\max(1,\sum_{e,t,i}m_e)}
\sum_{e,t,i}m_e
\left[
w_R d_{SO(3)}(\hat{R}_{e,t,i},R_{e,t,i})
+ w_x \|\hat{x}_{e,t,i}-x_{e,t,i}\|_1
\right],
\end{equation}
where \(d_{SO(3)}\) is the geodesic rotation distance and \(x\) denotes
translation.  The loss is semi-supervised because it can be evaluated on any
chosen fraction of labeled episodes while the rest of the model still
receives action and, when enabled, NVS gradients.

The estimated pose is then turned into dense camera geometry.  For each pixel
coordinate \(u\), intrinsics \(K_i\) and pose \(\hat{T}_{t,i}\) define a ray
origin \(o_{t,i}(u)\) and direction \(d_{t,i}(u)\).  We encode these rays as a
six-channel Plucker ray map~\citep{plucker1865newgeometry,sitzmann2021lfn}
\begin{equation}
P_{t,i}(u)=\left[o_{t,i}(u)\times d_{t,i}(u),\ d_{t,i}(u)\right].
\end{equation}
This token-level camera encoding follows the ray-map conditioning used in
recent view-synthesis transformers, where pixel-aligned rays provide the model
with camera geometry at the image-token level~\citep{jin2024lvsm,jiang2025rayzer}.
In robot learning, calibrated Plucker camera maps have been used to condition
RGB policies for viewpoint generalization~\citep{jiang2025knowyourcamera}.
Attention-level alternatives such as GTA and PRoPE instead inject relative
camera geometry inside self-attention~\citep{miyato2024gta,li2025prope}.  Our
main policy variants use the explicit Plucker form because it gives a direct
differentiable path from predicted pose to every visual token.  Because ray
construction is differentiable with respect to \(\hat{T}_{t,i}\), downstream
losses can update the pose head even when an image comes from an episode with
no camera-pose annotation.

\subsection{E2E Camera-Pose Action Policy}

The action-only variant tests whether action supervision and sparse,
episode-level pose labels are enough to learn useful camera geometry.  For each
view, we concatenate RGB and predicted ray features and tokenize the result with
an observation encoder:
\begin{equation}
z_t = f_\theta\left(\{I_{t,i}, P_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}, q_t, c\right).
\end{equation}
An action expert \(g_\psi\) predicts a chunk of future robot actions using a
conditional flow-matching decoder.  Let \(x_1=A_t\) be the demonstration action
chunk, \(x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), and \(\tau\sim U[0,1]\).  We train the
decoder to predict the velocity field along the interpolation
\begin{equation}
x_\tau = (1-(1-\sigma_{\min})\tau)x_0+\tau x_1,\qquad
u_\tau = x_1-(1-\sigma_{\min})x_0.
\end{equation}
The action loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}} =
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\tau}
\left[
\|g_\psi(x_\tau,\tau,z_t)-u_\tau\|_2^2
\right].
\end{equation}
The total objective for this variant is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{E2E-act}} =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}}+\lambda_{\text{pose}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}}.
\end{equation}
At inference, no ground-truth camera pose is provided.  The model predicts
camera poses from RGB, builds ray features from the predictions, and samples an
action chunk from the action decoder.

\subsection{E2E Camera-Pose NVS Policy}

The second variant adds an explicit 3D representation pressure through
scene-token novel-view synthesis.  This model uses a separate observation
encoder from the action-only variant.  The encoder maps source views and their
predicted ray features into a fixed set of latent scene tokens:
\begin{equation}
R_t = F_\theta\left(\{I_{t,i},P_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\right),
\qquad R_t\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}.
\end{equation}
This follows the set-latent scene representation used in view-synthesis
transformers~\citep{sajjadi2022srt,jin2024lvsm,jiang2025rayzer}.  In our
implementation, the encoder appends learned active scene tokens to the
multi-view patch sequence.  After transformer self-attention, the view patch
tokens are discarded and only the active scene tokens are retained.  The NVS
branch therefore decodes from a latent scene-token bottleneck rather than
directly from the full source patch sequence.  In the current implementation
\(M=64\) active scene tokens.  The action expert uses these tokens together
with proprioception and task conditioning:
\begin{equation}
z_t^{\text{scene}} = \text{concat}(R_t, q_t, c), \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}} =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}}(g_\psi, z_t^{\text{scene}}, A_t).
\end{equation}

For the NVS objective, we randomly select a target camera \(j\) and provide the
decoder with the target pose and intrinsics through target-view Plucker rays.
To prevent trivial copying, the target view's encoder input is masked with
probability \(p_{\text{drop}}\), so the decoder must reconstruct the target
image from the remaining scene evidence stored in the scene tokens.  The NVS
decoder \(D_\omega\) patchifies the target-view ray map, uses those target-ray
tokens as queries, and cross-attends only to the retained scene tokens:
\begin{equation}
\hat{I}_{t,j} =
D_\omega\left(R_t, P_{t,j}\right).
\end{equation}
We train it with a pixel and perceptual reconstruction loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{nvs}} =
\|\hat{I}_{t,j}-I_{t,j}\|_2^2
+\beta\,\text{LPIPS}(\hat{I}_{t,j}, I_{t,j}).
\end{equation}
This objective forces the scene tokens to encode object layout, occlusion, and
camera geometry rather than only action-correlated image features.  The complete
training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NVS}} =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}}
+\lambda_{\text{pose}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}}
+\lambda_{\text{nvs}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{nvs}}.
\end{equation}

The NVS decoder is used only during training.  At inference, the policy keeps
the pose head, scene-token observation encoder, and action expert, but it discards
the reconstruction head.  This preserves the end-to-end action policy interface:
the robot receives only RGB, proprioception, and task conditioning, while camera
geometry is inferred internally.
