\subsection{LIBERO-MV Spatial}

\paragraph{Goal and protocol.}
The LIBERO-MV Spatial experiment isolates how much camera-pose supervision is
needed.  We follow the LIBERO-MV Spatial protocol of Camera-Aware Multi-View
Visuomotor Policies~\citep{anonymous2026cameraaware}: each episode is re-rendered
with 12 surround cameras, the policy receives two random surround views, and
evaluation runs all 10 tasks at 50 rollouts each (500 per method).  Remaining
setup (resolution, action space, replanning) is in
\autoref{sec:appendix-implementation}.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
The reference methods share our backbone and action decoder and change only how
camera geometry enters the encoder: \texttt{ViT+CamID} uses a learned camera
identity with no metric geometry, \texttt{Plucker-GT} concatenates calibrated
Plucker ray maps to RGB, and \texttt{PRoPE-GT} injects calibrated relative pose
inside attention; all run under the same protocol as our policies.  Our E2E
camera-pose action policy instead predicts pose from RGB and trains the pose
head jointly with the action objective, with episode-level pose labels.

\paragraph{Pose-label sweep.}
We vary the fraction of pose-labeled training episodes over
\(\{10,30,50,80,100\}\%\).  The labeled subset is selected by a deterministic
episode-level split seed, and unlabeled episodes remain in the behavior-cloning
dataset.  Fig.~\ref{fig:libero} shows two trends.  First, a small amount of pose
supervision is already useful: the 10\% setting already exceeds the calibrated
Plucker baseline (0.820 vs.\ 0.622) and approaches the stronger calibrated PRoPE
reference (0.842).  Second, although our ray construction follows
the Plucker representation, and the calibrated Plucker baseline is weaker than
PRoPE on Spatial, end-to-end success stays high across the sweep (0.78--0.87),
with the best setting matching the calibrated PRoPE reference; given single-seed
noise we do not read the 50\% value as a true optimum.  This suggests that
action loss can shape predicted geometry toward
what the policy needs for control, rather than merely reproducing the calibrated
geometry used by a fixed encoder.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/libero_mv_spatial_summary.png}
  \caption{LIBERO-MV Spatial summary. Left: calibrated references from the Camera-Aware Multi-View Visuomotor Policies protocol and the best E2E camera-pose action policy. Right: episode-level success as the fraction of camera-pose-labeled training episodes varies.}
  \label{fig:libero}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Coupled vs.\ decoupled geometry.}
A natural alternative is to learn camera geometry \emph{outside} the control
objective: train a pose head in isolation by supervised regression of the
calibrated extrinsics, then plug its predictions into a frozen geometry-trained
policy.  This is a deployment-consistency test rather than a fully controlled
isolation of joint optimization: the frozen policy is trained on ground-truth
rays but deployed with predicted rays, while the coupled policy is trained and
evaluated on its own predicted geometry.  Under the identical LIBERO-MV Spatial
protocol, the decoupled two-stage variant reaches \(0.660\) success using
\emph{full} pose supervision, whereas our coupled E2E camera-pose action policy
reaches \(0.865\) using pose labels for only \(50\%\) of episodes --- a
\(20.5\)-point gain at half the label budget --- even though the decoupled pose
head is metrically far more accurate (\(0.33^\circ\) vs.\ \(2.42^\circ\)
rotation error; per-ratio numbers in \autoref{tab:appendix-pose-sweep}).

The label-ratio sweep provides a complementary within-coupled view.  Each
coupled run trains and evaluates with its own predicted rays, so it removes this
train--test geometry mismatch (though label-subset, single-seed, and
training-noise variation remain).  Across the sweep, reducing rotation error
from \(7.6^\circ\) to \(0.16^\circ\) changes Spatial success by under five
points, suggesting that within end-to-end training, lower standalone pose error
is not the main determinant of control performance: the policy instead learns
geometry that is useful for, and consistent with, the control objective.

\subsection{MimicGen Camera-Shift Evaluation}

\paragraph{Dataset and splits.}
We use MimicGen~\citep{mandlekar2023mimicgen} as a second simulation benchmark
because its generated demonstrations vary more episode-to-episode than
LIBERO-MV, a stronger test under camera shift.  We report four tasks of
increasing difficulty --- Stack (near-saturated), Stack Three, Coffee, and
Threading --- trained on the corresponding multicamera demonstrations (12
surround cameras per episode, 5\% held out by a fixed seed).

\paragraph{Camera distributions.}
Input cameras are sampled from a \texttt{train\_wide} distribution at training;
we evaluate on both \texttt{train\_wide} and a held-out \texttt{eval\_tight}
distribution whose surround cameras span a narrower angular range than training
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), separating in-distribution learning from
camera-distribution transfer (40 rollouts per setting).  We report a single
validation-selected checkpoint for both camera settings; selection details are
in \autoref{sec:appendix-implementation}.

\paragraph{Methods and inputs.}
We compare against Diffusion Policy~\citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy} as the image
baseline and ManiFlow~\citep{yan2025maniflow} as the point-cloud baseline.  We
follow the original hyperparameters as closely as possible for both baselines;
for ManiFlow, we use 1024 points to provide a higher-resolution point-cloud
input.  The two proposed methods are the E2E camera-pose action policy and the
E2E camera-pose NVS policy described in the Method section and
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}; implementation hyperparameters and rollout-reporting
details are summarized in \autoref{sec:appendix-implementation}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:mimicgen-results} reports the four-task result set.  The
final group reports a macro-average over the displayed tasks.  The NVS policy
improves over the E2E camera-pose action policy by 13.1 percentage points on
train-camera evaluation and 23.1 points under tight-camera evaluation.  The gain
in both regimes indicates that NVS is a general representation regularizer, not
only a camera-shift fix: even under the training camera distribution it forces
the scene tokens to encode object layout and occlusion rather than
action-correlated appearance shortcuts, which raises in-distribution success;
the larger tight-camera gain shows the benefit grows when those appearance
shortcuts break as the camera moves.  On Stack, it matches the action-only
variant on train-camera evaluation at 97.5\% success and improves tight-camera
success from 2.5\% to 42.5\%.  Relative to the point-cloud ManiFlow baseline,
the comparison is mixed rather than dominant: averaged over the four tasks our
NVS policy leads on train-camera success (46.9\% vs.\ 34.4\%) but trails on
tight-camera success (23.8\% vs.\ 31.9\%), so we do not claim to beat depth-based
point clouds under camera shift --- only to be competitive from RGB alone,
without depth or calibration at inference.

\paragraph{Ablations.}
Ablations on Stack Three (\autoref{fig:mimicgen-ablations}) support both
components: adding E2E pose prediction lifts train-camera success from 17.5\% to
30\%, and adding NVS reaches 55\% train / 25\% tight; removing the scene-token
bottleneck leaves train-camera unchanged (55\%) but halves tight-camera success
(25\%~$\to$~7.5\%), so the bottleneck matters most under camera shift.

\subsection{Real-World Tabletop Evaluation}

\paragraph{Protocol and supervision budget.}
We evaluate on a single-arm Trossen WidowX AI (one wrist and two scene-side
RealSense cameras).  The comparison is \emph{matched in protocol}: our policy and
both references use the same robot, camera rig, per-task rubrics, trial counts,
and success definitions, and differ only in how camera geometry is obtained ---
so this is not a cross-paper mix of different hardware, tasks, or scoring rules.
The Plucker-GT and Plucker-RayZer reference numbers are the calibrated and
decoupled baselines under this protocol on the same
hardware~\citep{anonymous2026cameraaware}; our E2E camera-pose NVS policy uses
pose labels for only 50\% of training episodes and no calibration at inference.
Each cell reports full-task success / rubric-weighted progress; rubric and
action-space details are in \autoref{sec:appendix-implementation}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:realworld} compares our coupled NVS policy against two references
on the same platform: a calibrated Plucker-GT upper bound that uses full
per-episode calibration, and a decoupled Plucker-RayZer baseline that --- like
ours --- predicts camera pose at inference, but trains the pose head in
isolation from a frozen policy.  Cup Stacking and Stand-up Cup use 20 trials
each and Blocks-in-Bowl uses 10.  Against the decoupled, calibration-free
baseline --- the natural apples-to-apples comparison --- our policy is at least
as strong on every task and wins decisively on two (Stand-up Cup 75 vs.\ 55,
Blocks-in-Bowl 100 vs.\ 30), with a narrow one-trial edge on Cup Stacking
(85 vs.\ 80, i.e.\ $17/20$ vs.\ $16/20$); the mean gap is $+31.7$ points,
mirroring the coupled-versus-decoupled gap seen on LIBERO-MV.  It also exceeds
the calibrated Plucker-GT reference in aggregate ($86.7$ vs.\ $70.0$ mean
success) despite using only half the pose labels and no inference calibration,
though this aggregate is driven mainly by Blocks-in-Bowl ($100$ vs.\ $50$);
on Cup Stacking the calibrated reference is saturated at $100\%$ and wins.

\paragraph{Scope.}
These are controlled point comparisons on a fixed real-robot task set with
small per-task trial counts, not a broad real-world generalization claim.  The
takeaway is specific: under a strictly weaker geometric-supervision budget and
no inference-time calibration, the learned-geometry policy matches or exceeds a
calibrated geometry-aware reference on the same hardware and rubric.
