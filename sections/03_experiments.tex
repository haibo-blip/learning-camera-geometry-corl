\subsection{LIBERO-MV Spatial}

\paragraph{Goal and protocol.}
The LIBERO-MV Spatial experiment isolates how much camera-pose supervision is
needed for end-to-end camera-pose learning.  We vary the fraction of training
episodes with pose labels and compare against policies trained with ground-truth
camera geometry.  To make this comparison meaningful, we follow the same
LIBERO-MV Spatial setting as Camera-Aware Multi-View Visuomotor
Policies~\citep{anonymous2026cameraaware}.  LIBERO-MV re-renders each LIBERO
episode with 12 static scene cameras sampled from the upper hemisphere around
the workspace.  During training and evaluation the policy receives two random
surround views.  Images are resized to
\(128{\times}128\), actions are 32-step chunks in an end-effector action space
with 6D rotation and gripper command, and the policy replans every 8
steps.  After training, evaluation runs all 10 LIBERO-MV Spatial tasks with 50
rollouts per task, for 500 rollouts per method.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
The reference methods share the same policy backbone and action decoder but
change how camera geometry enters the observation encoder.  \texttt{ViT+CamID}
uses RGB with a learned camera identity but no metric camera geometry.
\texttt{Plucker-GT} concatenates calibrated per-pixel Plucker ray maps to RGB.
\texttt{PRoPE-GT} injects calibrated relative camera pose inside attention.  All
calibrated baselines are run under the identical LIBERO-MV Spatial protocol used
for our policies --- same backbone, action decoder, camera sampling, resolution,
and rollout budget --- so the only changing factor is how camera geometry enters
the encoder.  Our
E2E camera-pose action policy replaces calibrated extrinsics with poses predicted
from RGB, constructs Plucker rays from those predictions, and trains the pose
head jointly with the action objective.  Pose supervision is assigned at the
episode level: an episode either provides all camera-pose labels or contributes
no pose loss.  Episodes without pose labels still train the action policy.

\paragraph{Pose-label sweep.}
We vary the fraction of pose-labeled training episodes over
\(\{10,30,50,80,100\}\%\).  The labeled subset is selected by a deterministic
episode-level split seed, and unlabeled episodes remain in the behavior-cloning
dataset.  Fig.~\ref{fig:libero} shows two trends.  First, a small amount of pose
supervision is already useful: the 10\% setting already exceeds the calibrated
Plucker baseline (0.820 vs.\ 0.622) and approaches the stronger calibrated PRoPE
reference (0.842).  Second, although our ray construction follows
the Plucker representation, and the calibrated Plucker baseline is weaker than
PRoPE on Spatial, end-to-end success stays high across the sweep (0.78--0.87),
with the best setting matching the calibrated PRoPE reference; given single-seed
noise we do not read the 50\% value as a true optimum.  This suggests that
action loss can shape predicted geometry toward
what the policy needs for control, rather than merely reproducing the calibrated
geometry used by a fixed encoder.

  \caption{LIBERO-MV Spatial summary. Left: calibrated references from the Camera-Aware Multi-View Visuomotor Policies protocol and the best E2E camera-pose action policy. Right: episode-level success as the fraction of camera-pose-labeled training episodes varies.}

\paragraph{Coupled vs.\ decoupled geometry.}
A natural alternative is to learn camera geometry \emph{outside} the control
objective: train a pose head in isolation by supervised regression of the
calibrated extrinsics, then plug its predictions into a frozen geometry-trained
policy with no joint optimization.  This policy is trained on ground-truth poses
and only sees the head's predictions at deployment, so it also incurs a
train-test pose-distribution shift --- which, as we discuss below, is precisely
what end-to-end coupling removes.  Under the identical LIBERO-MV Spatial
protocol, this decoupled two-stage variant (the predicted-pose deployment of the
calibrated Plucker policy) reaches \(0.660\) success using \emph{full} pose
supervision, whereas our coupled E2E camera-pose action policy reaches
\(0.865\) using pose labels for only \(50\%\) of episodes --- a \(20.5\)-point
gain at half the label budget.  Notably, the decoupled pose head is
\emph{metrically far more accurate} --- \(0.33^\circ\) rotation error versus
\(2.42^\circ\) for the coupled 50\% run --- yet yields the weaker policy
(Table~\ref{tab:pose-dissociation}).  The same pattern holds across the label
sweep (Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pose-sweep}): driving the coupled pose head from
\(7.6^\circ\) error at 10\% labels down to \(0.16^\circ\) at 100\% --- a roughly
\(48\times\) reduction --- moves Spatial success by under five points, and the
decoupled head, more accurate than every partially-labeled coupled run, still
gives the lowest success of all.  The sweep evidence is confound-free: each
coupled run is both trained and evaluated on its own predicted poses, so the
flat success despite a \(48\times\) error change isolates the effect cleanly.
The mechanism the two results point to is consistency rather than accuracy:
end-to-end coupling trains the policy on the same predicted geometry it uses at
test, so it tolerates a less metrically accurate but in-distribution pose,
whereas a decoupled policy trained on ground-truth poses is sensitive to the
predicted-pose shift even when those predictions are more accurate.  What helps
control is therefore geometry that is shaped by --- and consistent with --- the
control objective, not a metrically optimal pose.

\subsection{MimicGen Camera-Shift Evaluation}

\paragraph{Dataset and splits.}
We use MimicGen~\citep{mandlekar2023mimicgen} as a second simulation benchmark
because its generated demonstrations have larger episode-to-episode variation
than LIBERO-MV, making it a stronger test of policy performance under camera
shift.  We report four tasks with different difficulty profiles:
Stack is the easiest and is near-saturated for several methods; Stack Three adds
a longer multi-object stacking sequence; Coffee and Threading require more
precise object alignment and contact.  Each policy is trained on the
corresponding MimicGen multicamera demonstrations with 12 random surround
cameras available per episode.  We hold out 5\% of demonstrations with a fixed
split seed and train on the remaining demos.

\paragraph{Camera distributions.}
During training, input cameras are sampled from the \texttt{train\_wide}
distribution illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}.  We launch rollout
evaluation every 50k optimization steps on two camera settings: the same
\texttt{train\_wide} distribution used for training, and a held-out
\texttt{eval\_tight} distribution that draws the surround cameras from a
narrower angular spread clustered closer to a frontal band rather than the full
upper hemisphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), so the policy must transfer to
viewpoints outside the training distribution.  Each evaluation uses 40 rollouts
per camera setting.  This separates in-distribution policy learning from
camera-distribution transfer.

\paragraph{Methods and inputs.}
We compare against Diffusion Policy~\citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy} as the image
baseline and ManiFlow~\citep{yan2025maniflow} as the point-cloud baseline.  We
follow the original hyperparameters as closely as possible for both baselines;
for ManiFlow, we use 1024 points to provide a higher-resolution point-cloud
input.  The two proposed methods are the E2E camera-pose action policy and the
E2E camera-pose NVS policy described in the Method section and
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}; implementation hyperparameters are deferred to the
appendix.

\paragraph{Results.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:mimicgen-results} reports the four-task result set.  The
final group reports a macro-average over the displayed tasks.  The NVS policy
improves over the E2E camera-pose action policy by 13.1 percentage points on
train-camera evaluation and 23.1 points under tight-camera evaluation.  The gain
in both regimes indicates that NVS is a general representation regularizer, not
only a camera-shift fix: even under the training camera distribution it forces
the scene tokens to encode object layout and occlusion rather than
action-correlated appearance shortcuts, which raises in-distribution success;
the larger tight-camera gain shows the benefit grows when those appearance
shortcuts break as the camera moves.  On Stack, it matches the action-only
variant on train-camera evaluation at 97.5\% success and improves tight-camera
success from 2.5\% to 42.5\%.  Relative to the point-cloud ManiFlow baseline,
the comparison is mixed rather than dominant: averaged over the four tasks our
NVS policy leads on train-camera success (46.9\% vs.\ 34.4\%) but trails on
tight-camera success (23.8\% vs.\ 31.9\%), so we do not claim to beat depth-based
point clouds under camera shift --- only to be competitive from RGB alone,
without depth or calibration at inference.

\paragraph{Ablations.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:mimicgen-ablations} isolates two design choices on Stack Three.
Removing both end-to-end camera-pose prediction and NVS gives 17.5\% success on
train-camera evaluation and 0\% under tight-camera evaluation.  Adding
end-to-end pose prediction improves train-camera success to 30\%, while adding
NVS raises performance to 55\% train-camera and 25\% tight-camera success.  For
the NVS model, removing the scene-token bottleneck keeps train-camera success at
55\% but reduces tight-camera success from 25\% to 7.5\%, suggesting that the
latent scene-token bottleneck is most helpful under camera shift.

\subsection{Real-World Tabletop Evaluation}

\paragraph{Protocol and supervision budget.}
We evaluate on a single-arm Trossen WidowX AI in an ALOHA-style
leader--follower setup, with one wrist Intel RealSense D405 camera and two
scene-side RealSense D455F cameras.  Policies train from teleoperated episodes
resampled to a 50 Hz state/action clock, predict 1-second action chunks, and
represent each action as position, 6D rotation, and continuous gripper command.
The comparison is \emph{matched in protocol}: our policy and both references use
the same robot platform, the same wrist-plus-two-surround camera rig, and the
identical per-task rubrics, trial counts, and success definitions, and differ
only in how camera geometry is obtained.  The Plucker-GT and Plucker-RayZer
reference numbers are the calibrated and decoupled baselines evaluated under this
protocol on the same hardware~\citep{anonymous2026cameraaware}.  The calibrated Plucker-GT reference
consumes scene-camera extrinsics on every episode at both training and test
time; the decoupled Plucker-RayZer reference predicts pose at inference from a
separately trained pose head plugged into a frozen policy; our E2E camera-pose
NVS policy uses camera-pose labels for only 50\% of training episodes and no
calibration at inference, predicting camera geometry inside the policy.  Each
cell reports full-task success and
rubric-weighted task progress over the stated trial count.  For Blocks-in-Bowl
the trial has a 4-minute wall-clock cap, and the rubric gives one point per
successful grasp and one point per successful placement across four blocks
(eight points per trial; full success requires all four blocks placed).

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:realworld} compares our coupled NVS policy against two references
on the same platform: a calibrated Plucker-GT upper bound that uses full
per-episode calibration, and a decoupled Plucker-RayZer baseline that --- like
ours --- predicts camera pose at inference, but trains the pose head in
isolation from a frozen policy.  Cup Stacking and Stand-up Cup use 20 trials
each and Blocks-in-Bowl uses 10.  Against the decoupled, calibration-free
baseline --- the natural apples-to-apples comparison --- our policy is at least
as strong on every task and wins decisively on two (Stand-up Cup 75 vs.\ 55,
Blocks-in-Bowl 100 vs.\ 30), with a narrow one-trial edge on Cup Stacking
(85 vs.\ 80, i.e.\ $17/20$ vs.\ $16/20$); the mean gap is $+31.7$ points,
mirroring the coupled-versus-decoupled gap seen on LIBERO-MV.  It also exceeds
the calibrated Plucker-GT reference in aggregate ($86.7$ vs.\ $70.0$ mean
success) despite using only half the pose labels and no inference calibration,
though this aggregate is driven mainly by Blocks-in-Bowl ($100$ vs.\ $50$);
on Cup Stacking the calibrated reference is saturated at $100\%$ and wins.

\paragraph{Scope.}
These are controlled point comparisons on a fixed real-robot task set with
small per-task trial counts, not a broad real-world generalization claim.  The
takeaway is specific: under a strictly weaker geometric-supervision budget and
no inference-time calibration, the learned-geometry policy matches or exceeds a
calibrated geometry-aware reference on the same hardware and rubric.
