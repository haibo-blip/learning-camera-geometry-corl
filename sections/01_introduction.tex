RGB visuomotor policies have become strong action predictors: modern imitation
learning systems can map image observations and proprioception to action chunks
with diffusion or flow-style decoders~\citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy,yan2025maniflow}.
However, each image is also tied to a camera pose.  The same object relation can
project to different pixels as the camera moves, and different viewpoints expose
different occlusions, contact cues, and spatial relations.  Most image-based
policy recipes do not expose this geometry to the model: they feed RGB
observations directly to a policy and train it to predict actions.  In fixed or
narrow camera setups, such policies can solve demonstrations by exploiting
view-specific appearance cues, but these shortcuts need not transfer when camera
placement or evaluation views change.  This makes camera extrinsics an important
but unresolved design choice for policy encoders.

Existing systems make three different choices.  Image-only policies avoid
calibration, but must infer cross-view correspondence from pixels alone.
Camera-conditioned RGB policies provide geometry explicitly through calibrated
Plucker ray maps or related pose encodings~\citep{sitzmann2021lfn,anonymous2026cameraaware,li2025prope}.
Point-cloud and other 3D policy pipelines provide even stronger spatial
structure, but require depth, calibration, or a separate reconstruction
stage~\citep{ze2024dp3}.  These choices create a calibration gap.  The methods
that expose geometry to the policy usually assume the geometry is already known.
When a demonstration episode lacks camera extrinsics, calibrated ray and
point-cloud pipelines cannot construct their explicit geometry features for that
episode, even though the RGB observation and action label are still useful for
policy learning.

This paper asks whether camera geometry can be learned inside the control
objective rather than supplied as fixed metadata.  We treat camera pose as an
internal latent variable of the policy.  A pose predictor estimates each
camera-to-world transform from RGB, the predicted transforms are converted into
per-pixel ray features, and the downstream policy is trained end-to-end through
these features.  Camera-pose labels are only required for a chosen subset of
training episodes; all episodes still provide action supervision and can pass
gradients through the predicted geometry.  The central question is therefore not
whether a standalone pose head is metrically optimal, but whether partial pose
supervision plus task loss can turn unlabeled camera episodes into useful
training signal for control.

We study two model variants, summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}.  The
\emph{E2E camera-pose action policy} feeds RGB and predicted ray features into
an observation encoder and trains an action expert by behavior cloning.  The
\emph{E2E camera-pose NVS policy} uses a separate scene-token encoder and adds a
training-time novel-view synthesis objective.  Given a target camera query, an
NVS decoder reconstructs the target view from the scene tokens, following the
view-synthesis idea that renderable latent representations must preserve object
layout, occlusion, and camera geometry~\citep{sajjadi2022srt,jin2024lvsm,jiang2025rayzer}.
The decoder is discarded at inference; its role is to regularize the policy
representation during training.

Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates the training setting and the three
supervision signals.  We evaluate the models in simulation and in an initial
real-world tabletop run.  On LIBERO-MV Spatial~\citep{liu2023libero}, we vary
the fraction of episodes with ground-truth camera-pose labels and find that
partial supervision can be sufficient for strong control.  On MimicGen
tasks~\citep{mandlekar2023mimicgen}, we compare image, point-cloud, and
end-to-end camera-pose policies under both train-camera and tight-camera
evaluation.  Averaged across the four current MimicGen tasks, the NVS variant
improves over the action-only variant by 13.1 percentage points on
train-camera evaluation and 23.1 points under tight-camera evaluation.  This
shifts the story from pose prediction alone toward renderable scene
representations: target-view reconstruction appears to make the observation
encoder preserve geometry that the action objective can reuse.

The paper makes three contributions.  First, we formulate camera geometry
learning as an end-to-end visuomotor control problem with episode-level
semi-supervised camera-pose labels, so episodes without extrinsics still
contribute action and representation-learning gradients.  Second, we present two
camera-aware policy variants, one trained only with action and pose losses and
one regularized by scene-token novel-view synthesis.  Third, we provide initial
evidence on LIBERO-MV, MimicGen, and a real-world Blocks-in-Bowl task that
task-useful camera geometry can be learned inside the policy objective, reducing
reliance on fixed calibrated metadata at inference.
